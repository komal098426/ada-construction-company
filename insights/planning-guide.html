A Practical Guide to Planning Your Build

Most builds don't go wrong during construction. They go wrong in the gap between what was imagined and what was documented. This guide is how we close that gap.

Guide · ADA Construction Group Pty Ltd · [Publish date to be confirmed]

1. Know what you're solving for

Before plans, before builders, before budgets — write one paragraph describing what your current home can't do that your next one must. "More space" is a wish. "A home that fits our family of five and lets us work from home without converting the dining table" is a brief. Everything downstream — design, budget, service selection — gets easier once the brief is honest and specific.

2. Collect what you already hold

You'd be surprised how much useful information is sitting in your inbox or council's property portal. Gather what exists:

  • The title and any covenants or easements — these can quietly veto designs.
  • A recent survey — boundaries move in documents more often than on the ground.
  • Council property records — overlays (flood, bushfire, heritage) that shape what's approvable.
  • Any previous plans, approvals or permits for the site.

3. Understand the approval path before you design

The most expensive design is the beautiful one that can't be approved. Before engaging a designer deeply, understand — even roughly — which planning controls apply to your site and what they constrain. Your designer will know; a fifteen-minute conversation about overlays at the start is worth more than a redesign at the end.

4. Budget in ranges, then narrow honestly

Early budgets are ranges, and anyone quoting you a fixed price from a sketch is guessing with confidence. The useful sequence: establish the range your finances genuinely allow, share it with your designer (they can't design to a number they don't know), and narrow it as documentation firms up. A budget that's hidden from the people designing to it isn't a budget — it's a surprise waiting to happen.

5. Choose your builder before you need them

Good builders are booked ahead. The right time to start talking is during design development — not when drawings are finished and you're ready to sign. An early builder brings buildability into the design while changes are still cheap. Ask every builder you speak to the same three questions:

"What on this project worries you?" — every honest builder has an answer. "How do you handle surprises on site?" — the process matters more than the promise. "Who do I call during the build?" — you should get a name, not a role.

6. Settle the selections question

Nothing drags a programme like late selections. Tiles, tapware, joinery finishes, flooring — every item your builder must install needs a decision, and every late decision is a potential delay. Start your selections file early, even roughly; the point isn't to choose everything now, it's to know what needs choosing.

7. Plan the life-around-the-build

Where will you live during construction? What's your contingency if the programme moves — in either direction? Building is disruptive in planned ways; the families who enjoy the process most are the ones who planned the disruption rather than discovering it.
